Correct: \(x=-vt\), \(x'=0\).

For any object, its coordinate in a new frame is its old coordinate **minus the position of the new frame’s origin**.

**Lecture-slide question**

A point stays fixed at \(x=1\,\mathrm m,\ y=0\) in frame A. Frame B moves right at \(1\,\mathrm{m/s}\) relative to A, with their origins coinciding at \(t=0\).

What are the point’s \(x'\)-coordinates in B at \(t=0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3\,\mathrm s\)?

---

**Learner**

1,0,-1,-2

**Tutor**

Correct—all in metres.

That’s the **Galilean position transformation**:
\[
x'=x-vt.
\]
Here \(v\) is **B’s velocity relative to A**. The sign follows which frame is moving; primes alone don’t determine it. Classical physics also assumes \(t'=t\).

One more consequence from the slides: **inertial frames agree on acceleration**. Since their relative velocity \(v\) is constant,
\[
u'=u-v \quad\Rightarrow\quad a'=a.
\]
