Wait, compare and contrast Compton scattering and the... Compton scattering and the Doppler effect formulas. They both involve two different lambdas, and they both involve some sort of angle, right?

**Tutor**

Yes—both compare two wavelengths and involve an angle, but **the wavelengths and angles describe different things.**

| | Compton scattering | Relativistic Doppler effect |
|---|---|---|
| **What happens?** | A photon collides with an electron and transfers energy and momentum to it. | Relative motion changes the measured wavelength. |
| **Two wavelengths** | Before and after the collision, measured in the **same frame**. | The source-rest wavelength and the wavelength measured by another observer. |
| **Angle** | Between the photon’s **incoming and outgoing directions**. | Between a **relative-motion direction and the photon’s direction**; the exact convention matters. |

For **Compton scattering**, with the electron initially at rest:
\[
\boxed{\lambda_{\text{out}}-\lambda_{\text{in}}
=\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta)}
\]
Here \(\theta\) is the photon’s deflection angle. The wavelength increases or stays unchanged.

For **Doppler**, using the moving-source convention you practised:
\[
\boxed{\lambda_{\text{obs}}
=\lambda_0\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}
\]
Here \(\lambda_0\) is the source-rest wavelength, and \(\theta\) is measured in the observer’s frame between **the source’s velocity and the photon’s travel direction**. Depending on the geometry, the wavelength can increase or decrease.

The useful distinction: **Compton changes the photon through a collision; Doppler changes its measured energy through relative motion.** The angle in your current question is a scattering angle.

**Learner**

give a complete verbal discription of what and how the formula applies

**Tutor**

The formula
\[
\boxed{\lambda_{\text{obs}}=\lambda_0\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)}
\]
tells you **what wavelength an observer measures from a moving light source**, given the wavelength that source emits in its own rest frame.

Here is exactly what each quantity means:

- **\(\lambda_0\):** the wavelength measured by someone travelling with the source.
- **\(\lambda_{\text{obs}}\):** the wavelength measured by the receiving observer.
- **\(\beta=v/c\):** the source’s speed relative to the observer, divided by \(c\). Use a nonnegative speed; the angle accounts for direction.
- **\(\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\):** calculated from that same relative speed.
- **\(\theta\):** the angle between **the source’s velocity** and **the photon’s direction of travel toward the observer**, with both directions measured in the **observer’s frame**.

To determine the angle, imagine freezing the scene **at emission in the observer’s frame**. Draw two arrows starting at the source: one showing its velocity, and one pointing along the emitted photon’s path toward the receiver. The angle between those arrows is \(\theta\).

For Earth receiving light from a star:

- **Directly approaching:** the star moves in the same direction as the light travelling toward Earth. The arrows align, so \(\theta=0^\circ\), giving \(\lambda_{\text{obs}}=\lambda_0\gamma(1-\beta)\).
- **Directly receding:** the star moves opposite the light travelling toward Earth. The arrows oppose each other, so \(\theta=180^\circ\), giving \(\lambda_{\text{obs}}=\lambda_0\gamma(1+\beta)\).
- **Moving sideways:** the star’s velocity is perpendicular to the Earth–star line at emission. The photon travels along that line, so \(\theta=90^\circ\), giving \(\lambda_{\text{obs}}=\lambda_0\gamma\).

That Earth–star line is the **line of sight**. The phrase “perpendicular to the line of sight in Earth’s frame” therefore supplies the angle needed by this formula.

The crucial check is: **was the angle given in the receiving observer’s frame?** An angle measured in the source’s frame generally differs because light’s direction changes between frames. You cannot insert that source-frame angle directly into this version of the formula.

Finally, this describes a change in **measured wavelength due to relative motion**. The photon does not need to collide with anything or lose energy while travelling.

**Learner**

are all invariance questions really just E^2 Equals M squared c to the fourth plus p squared c squared?

**Tutor**

**For the energy–momentum problems we’ve been practising, mostly yes:**
\[
E^2-|\mathbf p|^2c^2=M^2c^4.
\]

The challenge is choosing **whose energy and momentum** you’re using:

- **One particle:** use that particle’s \(E\), \(\mathbf p\), and rest mass.
- **A whole system:** add the energies and **vector momenta first**, then apply the equation:
  \[
  E_{\text{tot}}^2-|\mathbf p_{\text{tot}}|^2c^2=M_{\text{system}}^2c^4.
  \]
  The system’s mass generally isn’t the sum of its particles’ masses—even two photons can have nonzero system mass.

Also distinguish two ideas:

- **Invariance:** the expression has the same value in every inertial frame, although \(E\) and \(\mathbf p\) change.
- **Conservation:** an isolated system’s total energy and momentum stay the same before and after a collision, using one frame.

Not *all* invariance questions concern energy–momentum. Spacetime questions use the invariant interval,
\[
c^2\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf x|^2.
\]

For your current collision problems, though, the recurring task is: **identify the system, add its energy and vector momentum, then apply the invariant.**
