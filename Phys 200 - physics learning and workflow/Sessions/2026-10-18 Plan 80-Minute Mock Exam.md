Yes—**for Q4, I would keep everything up to your final velocity calculation.** Your energy sum, momentum sum, and invariant calculation for the final mass are all appropriate.

You finished by solving
\[
P_{\text{tot}}=\gamma(V)MV.
\]
That works, but \(\gamma(V)\) also contains the unknown \(V\), so you have some algebra to do.

For the final combined object, you already know both:
\[
P_{\text{tot}}=\gamma(V)MV,
\qquad
E_{\text{tot}}=\gamma(V)Mc^2.
\]
Divide the two equations:
\[
\frac{P_{\text{tot}}}{E_{\text{tot}}}
=\frac{\gamma(V)MV}{\gamma(V)Mc^2}
=\frac{V}{c^2}.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{V=\frac{P_{\text{tot}}c^2}{E_{\text{tot}}}}.
\]

**The advantage is that both \(M\) and \(\gamma(V)\) cancel.** You can find the velocity directly from the conserved totals, without first finding the mass.

For your question, taking the first bullet’s direction as positive, the corrected totals are
\[
P_{\text{tot}}\approx-0.1099\,\mathrm{g}\,c,
\qquad
E_{\text{tot}}\approx8.0871\,\mathrm{g}\,c^2.
\]
So
\[
V=\frac{(-0.1099\,\mathrm{g}\,c)c^2}
{8.0871\,\mathrm{g}\,c^2}
\approx\boxed{-0.01359c}.
\]
The negative sign means the combined object moves in the second bullet’s original direction.

You would still use your invariant equation to find its rest mass:
\[
M=\frac{\sqrt{E_{\text{tot}}^2-P_{\text{tot}}^2c^2}}{c^2}.
\]

So this is **a shorter final step derived from equations you already used**, rather than a whole new method. It also lets you calculate mass and velocity separately from the same totals, so an arithmetic error in your mass won’t propagate into your velocity.

**Learner**

what is theta, there?

**Tutor**

In
\[
\lambda_{\rm obs}=\gamma\lambda_0(1-\beta\cos\theta),
\]
**\(\theta\) is the angle between the source’s velocity and the direction the light travels from the source toward the observer**, measured in the observer’s frame at emission.

- Source moving **toward** the observer: \(\theta=0^\circ\).
- Source moving **away**: \(\theta=180^\circ\).
- Source moving **perpendicular** to that direction: \(\theta=90^\circ\).

The direction matters: use **source → observer**, not observer → source.

**Learner**

Is there a rule to say whether beta is positive or negative?

**Tutor**

**In this angular formula, take \(\beta=v/c\geq0\): \(v\) is the source’s speed.** The angle accounts for its direction:
\[
\lambda_{\rm obs}=\gamma\lambda_0(1-\beta\cos\theta).
\]

- **Toward:** \(\cos\theta=+1\), so the factor is \(1-\beta\).
- **Away:** \(\cos\theta=-1\), so the factor is \(1+\beta\).
- **Perpendicular:** \(\cos\theta=0\), so the factor is \(1\).

**Don’t also make \(\beta\) negative for motion away**—that would count the direction twice.

In a Lorentz transformation, by contrast, \(\beta\) usually represents a **signed velocity along your chosen axis**. Its sign rule depends on which formula you’re using.
