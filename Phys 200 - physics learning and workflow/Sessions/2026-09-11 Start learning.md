**Learner**

Should I know where this formula comes from?

**Tutor**

**Yes—you should understand why it happens, rather than only memorize the formula.** I introduced it too abruptly.

Helliwell derives it using **time dilation**: observers disagree about the elapsed time for a moving clock to pass along a stick. Since both agree on their relative speed, their measured stick lengths must differ too.

Your course puts length contraction **before** time dilation, so we’re encountering that result before the textbook’s justification. For now, understand what the formula means and when it applies; once we’ve developed time dilation, we can derive it in a few lines.

That’s a learning recommendation—the reading scope doesn’t establish whether reproducing the derivation is examinable.

**Helliwell**

Barnard’s star is \(6.0\) light-years from the Sun in their shared rest frame. A spaceship travels toward it at \(0.8c\).

Using
\[
L=L_0\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}},
\]
how far apart are the Sun and star in the spaceship’s frame?

---

**Learner**

3.6 yr\*c

**Tutor**

Correct—\(3.6\,c\cdot\text{yr}\) is \(3.6\) light-years.

---

**Learner**

3.6 divided by 0.8? 4.5 years?

**Tutor**

Correct—\(3.6\) light-years divided by \(0.8c\) gives \(4.5\) years on the ship’s clock.
