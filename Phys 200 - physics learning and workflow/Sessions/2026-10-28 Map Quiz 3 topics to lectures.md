**Learner**

Why does the body have to be in thermal equilibrium to give off a spectrum of photons?

**Tutor**

**It doesn’t have to be in thermal equilibrium to emit photons or have a spectrum.** A hot object cooling down still emits radiation.

Equilibrium is what lets us describe the radiation using **the particular Planck spectrum associated with one temperature \(T\)**. Without equilibrium, an object can emit a different spectrum—for example, if different parts have different temperatures.

Also, an object needn’t be in equilibrium **with its surroundings** to emit approximately blackbody radiation. A hot object can be internally close to thermal equilibrium while slowly cooling in a colder room. Its spectrum then approximately follows the Planck curve at its current temperature.

The slide’s wording compresses that distinction.

**Learner**

How do I know these are black bodies?

**Tutor**

A **perfect blackbody absorbs all radiation incident on it**, reflecting or transmitting none. It also emits the ideal Planck spectrum at its temperature.

Real objects are usually **approximations**. You can’t conclude that something is a perfect blackbody just because it’s hot or glowing.

For this question, the clue is that the **light or warmth comes from the object’s temperature**. The Sun, hot burner, and hot coals are familiar thermal emitters, so the blackbody model helps explain them.

For the quiz, distinguish:

- **“Is it a perfect blackbody?”** You need information about its absorption or spectrum.
- **“Can blackbody radiation explain this phenomenon?”** Look for radiation emitted because something is hot.

And “black” doesn’t mean it must *look* black while hot—a blackbody can glow brightly.

What does thermal emitter mean? Emitting power because it has a certain temperature?

**Tutor**

Yes. A **thermal emitter** gives off electromagnetic radiation because it has a temperature. That radiation carries energy away; the energy emitted per second is its **radiated power**.

**Learner**

Yeah, the fact that electrons are ejected doesn't establish this, but K max equals hf minus W does, right?

**Tutor**

Yes—**the experimentally observed relation**
\[
K_{\max}=hf-W
\]
is evidence for the photon model: the maximum electron energy increases linearly with **frequency**, while increasing **intensity** at fixed frequency doesn’t increase \(K_{\max}\).

It fits the picture of one photon transferring energy \(hf\) to one electron, which spends \(W\) escaping.

**Learner**

that seems like the original EM wave is turned into the new one due to conservation of energy

**Tutor**

Yes—**the energy in the scattered wave comes from the incoming wave**, transferred through its interaction with the electron.

But the incoming wave isn’t necessarily completely converted: **some energy is scattered, while the rest continues past.** Energy conservation accounts for all of it.

Also, energy conservation alone doesn’t explain the unchanged frequency—that comes from the electron being driven at the incoming wave’s frequency.

**Learner**

not getting this

**Tutor**

Imagine **two identical waves starting together** at the two slits. Both travel to the **same point** on the screen, but one takes a longer route.

The extra distance determines how they line up when they meet:

- **Extra distance \(=\lambda\):** one wave is a whole cycle behind. A whole cycle brings you back to the same part of the wave, so **crest meets crest** → reinforcement → bright.
- **Extra distance \(=\lambda/2\):** one wave is half a cycle behind, so **crest meets trough** → cancellation → dark.

Think of two electric fields at that screen point:

\[
\text{Bright: } (+E)+(+E)=2E
\]
\[
\text{Dark: } (+E)+(-E)=0
\]

**We compare the distances travelled because they tell us whether the waves arrive lined up or opposed.**

Is the sticking point why an extra **half-wavelength** makes crest meet trough, or why that produces darkness?

**Learner**

so photons and matter, is what we have we talked about that are waves

**Tutor**

Yes—so far, we’ve discussed wave behaviour in:

- **Light (photons):** diffraction and interference.
- **Matter (electrons and even molecules):** diffraction and interference too.

Both also produce **individual, localized detections**. That combination is called **wave–particle duality**.
