**Learner**

Well, L naught is what's actually happening in the pion's frame. L without the naught is what we actually observe. So is my setup right?

**Tutor**

**No—the frame assignments are reversed here.** \(L_0\) means the distance measured in the frame where the two lab locations are at rest, so \(L_0=10.0\,\text{m}\).

In the pion’s frame, that lab distance is contracted: \(L=L_0/\gamma\). Pairing it with the pion’s lifetime gives
\[
v=\frac{L}{\Delta\tau}=\frac{L_0}{\gamma\Delta\tau}.
\]

The subscript \(0\) means **rest length**, not automatically “the pion’s frame.”

**Learner**

So it's asking how fast the pions are traveling? Why isn't it the length in its frame over its proper time?

**Tutor**

**Yes—that works.** You can use the distance in the pion’s frame divided by its proper lifetime:
\[
v=\frac{L}{\Delta\tau}
=\frac{10.0\,\text{m}/\gamma}{26.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{s}}.
\]

In that frame, the **lab moves past the stationary pion**. Its speed has the same magnitude as the pion’s speed in the lab.

Your approach was right; the correction was identifying **10.0 m as the lab-frame distance**, which must be contracted before dividing by the proper time.

**Learner**

How fast are the particles moving relatively To the laboratory. What does this mean? Does it mean from our perspective, how long it traveled over, how long it looks like it traveled, or the particles feels like it traveled over, how long it feels like it traveled, or something else?

**Tutor**

It means **the speed measured by someone at rest in the laboratory**:
\[
v=\frac{\text{distance traveled in the lab}}{\text{elapsed time measured by lab clocks}}.
\]

You can also calculate the **same speed magnitude** from the particle’s frame:
\[
v=\frac{\text{distance the lab moves past the particle}}{\text{elapsed time on the particle’s clock}}.
\]

**Both methods work. Keep the distance and time in the same frame.** Here, the given **600 m** is a lab measurement, while **2.0 μs** is a particle-clock measurement, so you cannot divide them directly.

“Measured” here means accounting for light-travel delays, rather than simply how things look through your eyes.

**Learner**

Before I hop in and answer. In general, if two things are moving to each other, they will not agree whether something happened at the same time, right?

**Tutor**

**Yes, generally—with an important distinction:** observers moving relative to each other can disagree about whether **two events at different locations** happened simultaneously.

They always agree if two events happen **at the same place and time**, such as the ship arriving at B and receiving the flash there.

In this question, **A and B are at rest relative to each other**, so their synchronized clocks share the same definition of simultaneity.
