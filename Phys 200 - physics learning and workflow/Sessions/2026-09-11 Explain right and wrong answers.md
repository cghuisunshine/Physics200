# Explain right and wrong answers

**Learner**

we need another inertial frame as a reference, perhaps

**Tutor**

Yes—**if we define it as “moving at constant velocity,” we need to specify: relative to another inertial frame.** Constant velocity relative to an arbitrary frame isn’t enough.

But we can identify an inertial frame by testing Newton’s first law: **does a free object—one with no net force—remain at rest or move at constant velocity?** If so, the frame is inertial.

Once we have one inertial frame, every frame moving at constant velocity relative to it is also inertial. There’s no special “absolutely stationary” frame.

The question implicitly treats the ground as approximately inertial and the cruising rocket as moving uniformly relative to an inertial frame.

---

**Learner**

oh, sitting on the rocket i feel a g force, samething i have to lean if im circling

**Tutor**

Yes—that’s the physical intuition.

- **Launching rocket:** the seat pushes you along with the accelerating rocket; you feel heavier.
- **Moving in a circle:** you need an inward force to keep changing direction. Leaning helps you balance while the ground supplies that force.

Those sensations indicate acceleration. **Cruising straight at a constant \(0.9c\)** produces no such sensation merely from the speed.

One subtlety: feeling a force alone isn’t conclusive—you also feel your chair while sitting still under gravity. Here, we’re identifying the extra effects from launching or turning.

---

**Learner**

give more examples

**Tutor**

Think about what happens to you or a loose object:

| Situation | Inertial? | What you notice |
|---|---|---|
| Train moving straight at constant speed | Approximately yes | A ball tossed straight up comes back to your hand. |
| Train speeding up | No | You feel pushed backward as the train accelerates beneath you. |
| Car braking | No | Your body continues forward; the seat belt slows you down. |
| Car rounding a bend at constant speed | No | Your body tends to continue straight; the car pushes you into the turn. |
| Elevator starting upward | No | The floor pushes harder on your feet; you feel heavier. |
| Elevator moving upward at constant speed | Approximately yes | You feel your usual weight, just as when stationary. |
| Spacecraft coasting with engines off, far from significant gravity | Yes, ideally | Loose objects initially at rest inside remain floating beside you. |
| Spacecraft spinning with engines off | No | Loose objects don’t keep rotating along with the cabin. |

**An elevator going upward but slowing down accelerates downward**—so you feel lighter. The direction you’re moving and the direction you’re accelerating can be different.

---

**Learner**

if i can feel the acceleration and rotation, im not in an inertial frame

**Tutor**

Yes—**if your frame is accelerating or rotating, it’s non-inertial.** Feeling pushed backward, sideways, or unusually heavy can help you recognize that.

But **the motion defines it, not whether you feel it**: very gentle acceleration can be imperceptible and still make the frame non-inertial.

---

**Learner**

yes, i would feel it if i were spiderman

**Tutor**

Exactly—with Spidey-level sensitivity, you’d notice even tiny accelerations or rotations. That’s the intuition: **steady straight-line speed feels like rest; acceleration or rotation can be detected.**
