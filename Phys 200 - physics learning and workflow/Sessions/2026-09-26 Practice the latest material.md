**Tutor**

Correct. Physically, **there’s no travel time available**: when A flashes, B also flashes 3 light-years away in Earth’s frame. A pilot beside A cannot also be beside B at that instant.

Changing frames doesn’t change whether the pilot actually encounters each flash. So no moving frame can put both flashes at one place.

These events are **spacelike-separated**: they can be simultaneous in a frame, but cannot share a position in any inertial frame.

**Learner**

Why are they called this?

**Tutor**

The names describe **which kind of separation can remain after changing frames**:

- **Timelike:** You can find a frame where the events happen at the **same place**, leaving only a time gap—like the two flashes beside the pilot’s seat.
- **Spacelike:** You can find a frame where the events happen at the **same time**, leaving only a spatial gap—like simultaneous flashes at two stations.
- **Lightlike:** A light pulse can connect the events. No inertial frame can eliminate either their spatial gap or their time gap.

So “timelike” means the separation can be purely temporal; “spacelike” means it can be purely spatial.
