**Learner**

What is polarization conceptually

**Tutor**

**Polarization describes the direction in which light’s electric field oscillates.**

Picture light travelling straight toward you. Its electric field oscillates **sideways—perpendicular to the direction the light travels**. It could oscillate up and down, left and right, or at some angle.

- **Linearly polarized light:** the electric field oscillates along one fixed direction.
- **Unpolarized light:** the oscillation direction varies randomly over time.

A **polarizing filter** lets through only the electric-field component along its transmission axis. An upright filter passes the up–down component; a horizontal filter passes the left–right component.

The key distinction: **polarization tells you how the field oscillates, not which way the light travels.**

---

**Learner**

We only filter electric field not magnetic ones?

**Tutor**

**The filter acts mainly on the electric field, but the magnetic field changes along with it.** They are coupled parts of the same light wave—you cannot transmit one while leaving the other behind.

We describe polarization using the electric-field direction by convention. Once that direction is set, the magnetic field must be perpendicular to both it and the direction of travel.

For example: light travelling toward you with an **up–down electric field** has a **left–right magnetic field**. Both pass through together.

How to write it so that it looks different than v

**Tutor**

Write **\(\nu\)** (“nu”) with a **rounded bottom** and a slightly curved right stroke, like a narrow \(u\). Write Latin **\(v\)** with a **sharp, pointed bottom**.

They can look very similar in handwriting—using \(f\) for frequency is also clear, unless you’re asked to use \(\nu\).

What does the second ask of a even mean?

**Tutor**

It’s asking **which speed to put into the time-dilation factor**:

- \(\gamma(v)\): the star’s **full speed**.
- \(\gamma(v\cos\theta)\): only its speed **toward the observer**.
- \(\gamma(v\sin\theta)\): only its speed **sideways relative to the observer**.

The answer is **\(\gamma(v)\)** because time dilation depends on the star’s total speed, regardless of its direction. You already chose correctly.

**Learner**

Why wouldn’t it be cos theta v?

**Tutor**

Because **sideways motion causes time dilation too**. The observer measures the star’s clock running slowly because the star is moving—not just because it’s moving toward them.

Imagine the star moving entirely sideways: \(\theta=90^\circ\), so \(v\cos\theta=0\). Using that would give \(\gamma(0)=1\), meaning **no time dilation**, even though the star is moving!

So there are two separate effects:

- **Time between emissions:** time dilation uses the full speed \(v\).
- **Travel time to the telescope:** motion toward the observer, \(v\cos\theta\), shortens the distance the later light travels.

Part (a) asks about the first effect.

**Learner**

Sound also has this right? Or does sound, as the say firetruck is right in front of you, sound correct even though it’s passing by

**Tutor**

**In the usual model for sound, purely sideways motion produces no Doppler shift.** So your intuition is right—with one timing detail.

For a passing firetruck:

- Approaching you → higher pitch.
- **Sound emitted at the closest point**, when its motion is perpendicular to your line of sight → the siren’s actual pitch.
- Moving away → lower pitch.

You hear each sound **after it travels to you**, so when the truck is directly in front of you, you’re still hearing sound emitted slightly earlier, while it was approaching.

For **(d)**, the key difference is that light has a transverse shift from **relativistic time dilation**. Ordinary sound calculations neglect time dilation; at firetruck speeds, it’s negligible.
